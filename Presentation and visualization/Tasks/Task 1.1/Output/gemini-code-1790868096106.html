2. Distributions, Groupings, and Relationships

An examination of the data's topological and statistical properties reveals distinct patterns critical for downstream modeling[cite: 1, 2]:

  • Spatio-Temporal Aggregation: Groupings must be handled hierarchically. Temporally, analyzing data at the daily level introduces high variance and zero-inflation; aggregating by week or month is necessary to extract underlying trend and seasonality components. Spatially, state-level aggregations mask critical intra-state variations; grouping by Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA) provides a more accurate reflection of incident density relative to population.
  • Overdispersed Distributions: The casualty fields (killed and wounded) are bounded at zero and heavily right-skewed. Rather than a standard Poisson distribution, these count metrics exhibit significant overdispersion (variance $\gg$ mean). They are best modeled using a Negative Binomial distribution, reflecting the underlying heterogeneity of mass casualty events where the baseline probability of an event occurring is decoupled from the conditional magnitude of the event once it begins.
  • Bivariate Relationships: The correlation between killed and wounded is positive but non-linear. The lethality ratio (Killed / Total Casualties) varies drastically based on the weapon utilized and the indoor/outdoor nature of the venue, serving as a latent variable that must be inferred through spatial contexts.

3. Missing Data, Incoherences, and Epistemological Limitations

A rigorous evaluation of data integrity highlights several structural challenges[cite: 1, 2]:

  • Missing Data Mechanisms: The core metrics (date, location, casualties) exhibit near-zero missingness because they dictate dataset inclusion. However, the shooters field is frequently missing. This is not Missing Completely at Random (MCAR); it is likely Missing Not at Random (MNAR), as incidents related to gang violence or immediate suspect evasion are disproportionately less likely to have confirmed shooter identities in initial reporting compared to high-profile lone-actor events.
  • Structural Incoherences: The primary incoherence stems from the dataset's operational ontology. The Mass Shooting Tracker defines a mass shooting strictly by a numeric threshold (4 or more people shot, regardless of fatalities), whereas historical FBI definitions focused on fatalities (3 or more killed). This definitional divergence means the dataset conflates highly disparate sociological phenomena—such as targeted public massacres, domestic violence events, and organized crime shootouts—under a single numerical umbrella.
  • Real vs. Forecast Data: The dataset relies strictly on empirical, ex-post reporting of real events. No forecast data or predictive intervals are present[cite: 1].

4. Outlier Analysis and Extreme Value Theory

Identifying and handling outliers in this dataset requires a domain-specific approach[cite: 1, 2]. In many data science contexts, extreme outliers (e.g., $z > 3$) are candidates for Winsorization or exclusion to normalize distributions. Here, extreme events (such as the 2017 Las Vegas shooting or the 2016 Orlando nightclub shooting) are the primary objects of interest.

These outliers exist in the extreme right tail of the distribution. Applying Extreme Value Theory (EVT), specifically the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD), is appropriate for modeling the tail behavior of these high-lethality events. Standard measures of central tendency (mean) are highly distorted by these outliers; therefore, exploratory reporting must rely on the median, Interquartile Range (IQR), and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) to describe typical events, while treating the long tail as a distinct, critical signal rather than noise.

5. Visualization Strategy for High-Dimensional Data

Based on this EDA, the final visualization architecture must move beyond simple bar charts to capture the multidimensionality of the data[cite: 2]:

  • Temporal Evolution: Ridgeline plots (Joyplots) will be utilized to show the distribution of incidents per month across the 2013-2025 timeline, effectively highlighting both increasing frequencies and seasonal shifts.
  • Geospatial Density: A bivariate choropleth map or Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) overlay on a US map will map incident frequency against state population density, normalizing the data per 100,000 residents to prevent the map from merely functioning as a population heatmap.
  • Casualty Disparities: Scatter plots representing wounded versus killed will utilize logarithmic scales on both axes to compress the extreme outliers, allowing for visual separation of typical incidents (clustered near the origin) from high-magnitude massacres, with bubble size mapped to total_victims.

Description: This matrix-style heatmap plots years along one axis and calendar months along the other, using color intensity to represent incident volume.
